<h3 style="color: #0D47A1;">Explore the Plural: Artists of Latin America exhibition and the artworks presented.</h3>

<p style="color: #17324D; text-indent: 30px; margin-bottom: 1.5rem;">
Julia Dawson’s photographs show the symposium’s panels on literature and art criticism and the diversity of the figures in conversation. In the photos taken at “Literary Criticism: Its Present Status in Latin America”, users can see literary critics from Brazil, Mexico, Peru, Chile, Argentina, and the United States, representing universities including the Catholic University of Rio de Janeiro, Indiana University, the University of Washington, and the University of Texas at Austin itself, in conversation with each other and with panel attendees. Dawson captured the speakers and audience members during the panel all engaged with the subject.  
Meanwhile, at the panel titled "With What Operative Models, Local and Imported, Does the Latin American Plastic Artist Face His Circumstances;  and With What Sort of Critical Feedback Is He Faced?", critics and directors representing a multitude of international universities, including Jorge Alberto Manrique of the National University of Mexico, Aracy Amaral of the University of São Paulo, Brazil, and panel chairman Donald B. Goodall of the University of Texas. They presented alongside artists including Rufino Tamayo from Mexico, Leonel Góngora of Colombia, and Alejandro Otero from Venezuela. As with the literary criticism panel, a variety of Latin American perspectives are brought together, but in the context of a United States university with United States faculty and educators. In context, outside influence is unavoidable. 
	The theme with the most items, Exhibition, covers the Plural galleries and the artworks they display. The Exhibition’s artworks (arranged into sections by artist with the artist’s name and country of origin as the title of each gallery) are framed against massive white walls, by themselves or alongside others, sometimes with the works of the other artists visible in the background, but always with the greater context of the exhibition in mind. The artwork represents a dozen painters and sculptors originating or working in Latin America, all of whom were selected by Plural Editor-in-Chief Kazuya Sakai and UT-Austin visiting Professor Damián Bayón. This group includes sculptors and painters, most originating in Latin America, but with Brian Nissen born in England, Roger von Gunten from Switzerland, and Vicente Rojo from Spain (the art criticism panel places Spain in the same cultural and critical set with Latin American countries, but Rojo’s presence still demonstrates outside influence), all living and working in Mexico. The choices represent a region pushing to define its own identity in terms of art as well as art criticism, yet with the influence of the outside world (and Europe in particular) never far away. The same story is visible stylistically, with local cultural influences and art movements like Neo-Concrete Constructivism blending with global styles like Kinetic Art, Cubism, and Abstraction.  
	Finally, the theme of Connection uses the exhibition’s reception to explore the interactions between artists, critics, and panel speakers. Kazuya Sakai converses with Donald B. Goodall, Rodolfo Cardona the Chairman and Professor of the Department of Spanish and Portuguese at UT-Austin interacts with guests alongside Aracy Amaral, and attendees of all kinds interact and share the experience of the symposium and exhibition with each other. Even without the context of the spoken word, communication is visible, guests smiling at Mexican artist Helen Escobedo, Donald B. Goodall shaking the hands of attendees. The art itself remains visible in some shots, the direct subject of guest attention and conversation in some shots, the subject matter of the exhibition in perpetual conversation with all involved. Here, the worlds of art, criticism, and wider cultural understanding mix, and while the meetings present may not have the structure of the symposium proper, they bear the potential for Latin American artistic identity alongside the seeming inevitability of outside influence. 

</p>
